My ex says the business is worth nothing. What should I do?

Short answerDon't accept it or reject it yet; check it. Some small businesses genuinely are worth little beyond their equipment, while others that look modest on paper are worth a good deal more. The figures usually tell you which, if someone who knows what to look for reads them.

The Necessity Report™ is a fixed-fee check of that question, $997 including GST. It does not put a value on the business; it tells you whether a valuation is worth paying for.

Why it might be true

A business can be worth very little if it is really one person's labour. When the owner is the business, and customers come only because of them, a buyer would be paying for a job rather than an asset, and that kind of goodwill often has little value in a property settlement. Even then, the business will usually own something: vehicles, tools, stock, money owed by customers, or cash in the bank.

Why it might not be

  • Profit is not the same as wages. If the business pays its owner a modest wage and still makes a profit on top, that extra may carry value of its own.
  • The accounts were prepared for tax. Financial statements are usually written to keep tax down, not to show what a business is worth, so private costs run through the business can make profit look smaller than it is.
  • Income dropped around the separation. A sudden fall can be real, since businesses do have bad years, though it is worth seeing whether the records explain it.
  • Money sits in a loan account. Amounts owed between the owner and the business, or a family trust, can change the picture considerably.

None of these means anyone is hiding anything; plenty of honest businesses look odd on paper. They are simply the points where "worth nothing" and the records sometimes part company.

What to do next

  1. Ask for the business's financial statements and tax returns for the last three years.
  2. Ask whether a company or family trust is involved, and for its accounts too.
  3. Have someone independent look at them before either of you pays for a full valuation, or agrees to a figure of nil.

If your ex will not provide the records, that is a disclosure question for your lawyer or mediator rather than an accounting one.
